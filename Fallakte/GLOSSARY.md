# Terms, defined once

Written 2026-10-10, after a table in the first case file used *relevant cells*
and *disease at birth* as though they were self-explanatory. They are not, and
**the reader coming into the field is the one the register is for.**

Every entry says what the term means **and what it does not mean**, because in
this vocabulary the near-miss is the problem rather than the blank.

**One rule for case files: if a term is in this list, use it as defined here. If
a case needs a term that is not here, add it here rather than redefining it
locally.**

---

## The four states a disease can be in, and they are not the same question

This is the distinction that caused the trouble. "Does the baby have the
disease?" is four questions, and for most genetic conditions the answers differ.

| state | the question | how you would know |
|---|---|---|
| **defect present** | is the broken instruction there? | sequence the gene |
| **cells misbehaving** | are the affected cells doing the wrong thing *now*? | measure what they make or clear |
| **detectable** | would an ordinary test on this person be abnormal? | run the test |
| **harming** | is the person being damaged? | look for the damage |

**They come apart in both directions.** A person can have the defect present and
nothing misbehaving, because too few of their cells carry it. A person can have
cells misbehaving and nothing detectable, for the same reason. And a person can
have everything detectable for forty years with nothing harmful yet, because the
harm is damage that accumulates.

**So "they are born with it" is an answer to the first question only**, and it is
the sentence that makes people expect a sick baby.

## Where a mutation comes from

**germline** — the mutation is in the sperm or egg the person was made from, so
**it is in every cell of their body**, and it was there before there was a body.
Inherited from a parent, and passed to about half their children.
*It does not mean* the disease is present at birth. It means the *defect* is.

**somatic** — the mutation happens in **one ordinary cell of a body that already
exists**, while that cell copies its own DNA. It is in that cell and in every
cell descended from it, and **in nothing else.** Not inherited and not passed to
children.
*It does not mean* "later in life". A somatic mutation can happen in a fetus, and
in polycythaemia vera it often does. **Somatic is about WHICH CELLS, not WHEN.**

**mosaic** — the state of a person who has a somatic mutation: **two genetically
different populations of cells in one body.** The normal population is not
repaired or protected. It simply was never copied from the mutated cell.

**clone** — one cell and all of its descendants. When a case file says *the
clone grew*, it means that cell's share of a tissue increased, not that the
mutation spread. **A mutation never spreads between cells. Only descent carries
it.**

## How much of a tissue the clone is

**relevant cells** — *retired. Do not use.* It was shorthand for "the cells whose
malfunction produces this phenotype", and it hid the fact that the answer differs
per disease. **Name the cell type.** For familial hypercholesterolaemia it is the
liver cells that clear LDL from blood. For polycythaemia vera it is the blood stem
cells in the marrow.

**clonal fraction** — the share of a tissue descended from the mutated cell. This
is the quantity that turns a mutation into a disease, and it is why a case file
can need a stock-and-flow model to say anything at all.

**allele burden** / **variant allele fraction (VAF)** — what is actually measured
in a blood sample: the fraction of gene copies carrying the mutation. **Not the
same as clonal fraction.** Each cell has two copies of the gene and usually only
one is mutated, so a clone that is 60% of the blood reads as about 30% allele
burden. A reading above half means some cells have lost their good copy.

**clonal haematopoiesis** — a detectable clone in the blood with no disease.
Common with age, and **the ordinary outcome of carrying one of these mutations**
rather than a rare near-miss.

## Why the harm is late, and the two reasons are different

**Both of these are delays between a defect and a harm, and confusing them is
what makes inherited and acquired disease look alike.**

**accumulation delay** — the cells misbehave from the start and the *damage* takes
decades to add up. Familial hypercholesterolaemia is this: cholesterol clearance
is impaired from infancy, the blood test is abnormal in childhood, and what takes
until the fifth decade is the artery.

**expansion delay** — the cells that misbehave are too few to matter, and what
takes decades is **the clone growing**. Polycythaemia vera has this delay, and
then the accumulation delay on top of it. **Two delays stacked**, which is why it
presents later than a germline disease of comparable severity.

## Iron, because it turns out to be the material and not a detail

**haem iron** — most of the iron in a body is inside haemoglobin, in circulating red
cells. **So blood is where the iron is**, and losing blood is losing iron. This is
the fact that makes venesection do two things at once.

**the iron loop** — iron is **recycled, not consumed.** A red cell lives about four
months, a macrophage eats it and recovers its iron, and the iron is used again. Diet
contributes a small fraction of the daily throughput. **The body is a nearly closed
loop with a small leak**, which is why repeated blood loss is expensive and why there
is no quick way to replace it.

**hepcidin** — the hormone that controls whether iron is *available*. High hepcidin
locks iron away. It is made in proportion to how loaded the iron stores are, and
removed in proportion to how hard the marrow is demanding iron.
*It does not mean* an iron transporter. Hepcidin is the signal; ferroportin is the
door.

**ferroportin** — the pore iron leaves a cell through, on macrophages and gut lining.
Hepcidin's whole job is to have it destroyed. **No ferroportin, no iron released**,
however full the stores are.

**erythroferrone** — the signal an over-driven marrow sends to suppress hepcidin, so
that demand opens the gate. It is why a marrow making too many red cells gets more
iron rather than less.

**absolute iron deficiency** — the stores are empty. There is no iron.

**functional iron deficiency** — **the stores are full and the iron cannot get out.**
A closed gate rather than an empty tank. The distinction matters because it is the
difference between what venesection does and what a hepcidin mimetic does, and the
second holds the haematocrit without stripping the patient.

**ferritin** — the blood test that estimates how much iron is in the stores. Low
ferritin is the usual marker of absolute deficiency.

## On this disease specifically

**polycythaemia** — too many red blood cells. A description, not a cause.

**polycythaemia vera** — the version caused by a clone in the marrow that makes
red cells without being told to. *Vera* means "true", which in practice
distinguishes it from the far commoner **secondary** polycythaemia, where the
marrow is normal and is responding correctly to a real signal, as at altitude or
in lung disease.

**erythropoietin (EPO)** — the hormone the kidney releases to tell the marrow to
make more red cells. **In this disease it is low**, because the kidney is working
and the marrow has stopped listening.

**haematocrit** — the percentage of blood volume that is red cells. The number
treatment is aimed at.

**myeloproliferative neoplasm** — the family this disease belongs to: a blood
cancer in which the marrow overproduces mature cells rather than failing to make
them.
